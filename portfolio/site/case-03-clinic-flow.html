Case study 03 · Healthcare operations

Where the Wait Actually Is

Everyone assumed the emergency department was slow. Nearly half of an admitted patient's time was spent waiting for an inpatient bed — after the ED had already finished with them.

  • Tableau
  • Python / pandas
  • Long→wide reshape
  • ~3 hours build

Data: CMS Timely and Effective Care – Hospital, the public facility-level file behind Hospital Compare. Real, public, updated quarterly.

The question

Where does patient time actually go, and which sites are losing patients to the wait? A patient who leaves without being seen is revenue gone and a clinical risk accepted. Sites track that rate. Few track it against the thing causing it.

What the file actually looked like

The mess here isn't dirty values. It's structural, and it's the kind that produces confidently wrong answers.

The bug I shipped, and caught

First run reported 120 facilities but produced 334 rows of pivoted output, and the derived boarding-share metric came out null across the board.

The pivot was keyed on facility ID plus city, county and ZIP. Those address fields drift between rows the same way the facility name does. So one hospital with three ZIP spellings became three pivot rows, each holding a fraction of its measures — which reads as more facilities, each reporting less data.

Nothing errored. The row count looked plausible. Every per-facility rate would have been quietly wrong.

The fix is the general rule: resolve every attribute to one value per entity key before you group or pivot anything — not just the label you happened to notice was dirty.

This is in the case study on purpose. A flawless narrative proves nothing; anyone can produce one by leaving things out. Showing a bug caught in my own pipeline demonstrates the thing a client is actually buying, which is that I check.

The decisions that mattered

“Not Available” is never zero

A suppressed walk-out rate is not a 0% walk-out rate. Zero-filling would rank the facilities with the worst reporting as the best performers — an inversion that destroys trust in a dashboard on first read. Missing values stay missing and display their reason.

The suppression reason is preserved, because it splits two findings

FootnoteCountMeaningIs it a finding?
3, 529Too few cases to report reliablyNo — a small rural site. Expected.
2520Facility did not submit dataYes — a compliance gap

Collapsing both into “missing” throws away the only interesting part. In a real engagement the did-not-submit list is usually the first thing the operations director asks for.

Units stay explicit

A unit column travels with every measure. Minutes and percentages never share an axis, and no aggregate is ever computed across measures of different units.

The recommendation. The derived metric — boarding time as a share of an admitted patient's total stay — reframes the entire conversation. At a median of 45%, the constraint isn't ED throughput. It's inpatient bed availability. That's a different problem, with a different owner and a different budget line, and no amount of ED staffing fixes it.

A derived metric that changes who owns the problem is what makes a dashboard worth paying for. Restating numbers the client already has does not.

What this dashboard refuses to say

Congestion and walk-out rates are associated in this data. That is not the same as one causing the other, and the footer says so. Case mix, acuity and staffing — the likeliest actual drivers — aren't in this file, so facilities are grouped by reported volume rather than ranked against each other. A trauma centre and a rural ED are not comparable on raw minutes.

Healthcare buyers have been burned by dashboards that zero-filled suppressed measures and by consultants who asserted causation from a scatter plot. Naming both limits unprompted is the credential.

Scope

About three hours, including the hour spent chasing the pivot bug. Reruns against each quarterly CMS release with one command.
